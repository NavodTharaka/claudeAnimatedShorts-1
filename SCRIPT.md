# "The Stars Speak" — original script v1 (2026-11-15)
Format: ~75 s vertical short, ~3.7 words/s, our own characters, story and voice.

## Facts (sources to verify before voice is recorded)
- Nebuchadnezzar II ruled Babylon 605-562 BCE; the Ishtar Gate was built around 575 BCE. (Wikipedia: Nebuchadnezzar II, Ishtar Gate)
- Babylonian scholars watched the sky from temple towers and reported omens to the king in letters. The omen series
  Enuma Anu Enlil held roughly 7,000 omens. (Wikipedia: Enuma Anu Enlil; Babylonian astronomy)
- Babylonians kept nightly Astronomical Diaries for centuries (from about 652 BCE) and learned to predict eclipses
  from the repeating Saros cycle of about 18 years. (Wikipedia: Astronomical Diaries; Saros)
- Substitute king ritual: when an eclipse omen threatened the king, a stand-in was crowned for a short time and then
  put to death so the omen fell on him. Best attested in Assyrian letters (Esarhaddon) and Mesopotamian practice;
  shown here as "the old custom", not as a proven Nebuchadnezzar event. (Wikipedia: Substitute king ritual)
- Reports to the king often ended with a calming line. The astrologer's own risk is dramatised: the Bible (Daniel 2)
  has Nebuchadnezzar threatening his wise men, so it is not claimed as record.
- Characters (Nabu-zer, the astrologer; the king) are invented.

## Script (about 270 words)
In ancient Babylon, the royal astrologer had one terrifying job: tell the king when the sky said he would die.
It's 575 BCE, and Babylon is at its proudest, with blue glazed gates and a tower to the heavens.
Every night, Nabu-zer climbs the tower with a clay tablet and a very steady hand.
He has watched the moon for years.
Tonight, he sees a dark bite taking the moon.
He checks his tablets. Then he checks them again. Then he sits down on the steps.
Because the old omens are clear: this eclipse means danger to the king.
Now he has to carry the news down to a king who has people executed for less.
The court goes silent. The king leans forward. Nabu-zer swallows, bows, and reads the omen out loud.
Luckily, the king did not panic.
Unluckily, his plan was to crown somebody else.
For a few days, a stand-in king sits on the throne, so the stars would aim at him instead.
But here is the clever part. Nabu-zer did not guess any of it.
Babylon had written down the sky every night for centuries, and eclipses come back in a cycle of about eighteen years.
It's like knowing the bus timetable: once you see the pattern, you know when the next one shows up.
The king stays alive, the stand-in gets a very nice few days, and Nabu-zer keeps his head.
And every report he wrote afterwards ended with the same four words: the king should not worry.
